🧠 What “Thinking, Fast and Slow” Taught Me About My Own Mind

— Notes from Thinking, Fast and Slow by Daniel Kahneman

Reading this book felt like being handed a mirror — not for my face, but for my thoughts.

Daniel Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow breaks the mind into two systems. System 1 is fast, intuitive, automatic. It helps us jump to conclusions, recognize faces, dodge danger. System 2 is slow, deliberate, logical. It kicks in when we do math, weigh options, or question our assumptions.

The problem? Most of the time, we think we’re using System 2, but it’s actually lazy. So we end up relying on quick judgments from System 1 — even when we shouldn’t.

I started noticing this in my everyday life. When I judge someone instantly. When I feel confident in a choice without checking the facts. When I assume a story is true because it “feels right.” These are all System 1 traps. The book calls them cognitive biases — shortcuts that save time but distort truth.

One that hit me hard was the “availability heuristic.” We think something is more likely just because it’s easier to remember. Shark attacks, plane crashes, lottery winners. They’re rare, but vivid — so we overestimate them.

Another is the “halo effect.” If we like one thing about a person — their smile, voice, or confidence — we assume everything else about them is good too. That explains a lot about bad hires, first dates, and political campaigns.

Kahneman doesn’t ask us to shut down System 1 — that’s impossible. What he urges is awareness. Noticing when we might be rushing. Slowing down when it matters. And most of all, being humble about how little we really know.

This book didn’t give me all the answers. But it gave me better questions. It taught me that my brain is brilliant — and flawed. That my confidence is not always a sign of correctness. And that sometimes, slowing down isn’t weakness. It’s wisdom.

If you’ve ever wondered why people (yourself included) make such strange choices, start here. It's not a fast read, but it’s one that stays with you — long after the last page.
